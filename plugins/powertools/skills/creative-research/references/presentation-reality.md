## You are not advertising to an audience

&gt; **You're making ads for a specific type of brain.**

That brain is inside an ecosystem purpose-built for mindless scrolling through topics of interest. It does not want to click out, evaluate your offer, and weigh your bells and whistles. That's an energy expenditure. **The brain is trying to keep itself where it is.**

Most creative teams choose their stack — how many statics, videos, carousels, GIFs — based on what's trending, what feels fresh, what the founder likes, or what the team is excited about. Excitement is fine. It's not a selection criterion.

**Choose the creative stack based on the psychological state of the person who will process the ad.** That's the only question here.

A brain in exploration mode rejects anything that gets too clever or goes too deep too fast. A brain in purchase mode resents a landing page that over-explains — *I'm past this, let me buy.* Match the information to the state, or it feels wrong to the brain and gets filtered.

## The TEEP framework

**T**rigger → **E**xploration → **E**valuation → **P**urchase

Drawn from Google research on digital purchasing processes. Customers move through four phases before buying, and they **morph in and out of exploration and evaluation** — sometimes for a long time — before dropping into purchase. Post-purchase experience (unboxing, shipping) loops back into the cycle.

Each phase has a distinct psychological state. Each state has formats that match it.

**Most brands run creative toward roughly two of the four phases.** Counting your ads by phase shows you instantly where you're over-indexed and what's missing.

## The four phases in depth

### Trigger — pre-awareness

**State:** They don't know they're in market. They haven't typed anything into a search bar. They're living moment to moment and don't know a solution exists.

**Your job:** Not to sell. **Name the moment.** Make them feel seen, not sold to.

**Formats:** The micro-moments you pulled in customer research. Highly relatable, "this is so me" content. Interesting data that makes someone stop and think *huh, that's my husband.*

**Expect them to leave.** Most people will stop on a trigger ad, think *I need to look at this later*, and scroll on. That's normal and correct. You catch them in the next phase.

**Keep a library** of ads that make people feel seen.

---

### Exploration — problem-aware

**State:** They can name the problem. They want it gone. They're curious and open to options — but not ready to watch a four-minute video of everything you have to say.

**Your job:** Earn the right to say more. **Talk about the industry, not about you.**

**Formats:** Short-form video, visual hooks, novelty, memes, ASMR, satisfying textures, pattern interrupts. Low commitment, high novelty. Content shouldn't feel heavy — it should feel like *this connects to me.*

**This is where generational insight pays off.** If this customer's generation trusts institutions, bring in a dermatologist. If it trusts peers, bring in a peer. You're not naming your brand yet; you're establishing that the category is worth their attention in the way *they* find credible.

---

### Evaluation — comparing options

**State:** They've moved from *is this possible* to *is this the right one for me.* Skeptical, but actively looking for evidence.

**Your job:** Now you talk about **us**. Give them everything they need to compare, and remove the risk of trusting you.

**Formats:** Testimonials, before/afters, educational content, case studies, comparison tables. This is where direct-response skill fully applies.

Comparison-table ads have been running successfully for six-plus years without failing. If you have data on why to choose you, put it here.

**Alignment matters here:** an evaluation ad communicating total *value* attracts a different customer than one communicating cheap *price*. Match it to the brand optimization statement.

---

### Purchase — ready

**State:** Credit card in hand. Waiting for a reason to act now.

**Your job:** **Let them buy.**

**Formats:** Offers, discounts, free gift, long-form, founder ads.

**The expensive failure:** someone is sitting there with a card, and you keep educating them. They wait. You keep talking. They walk away — *I was trying to buy and you didn't let me.*

You've already answered the questions. Offering something will not turn them away.

## Weighting by optimization goal

This is where presentation reality meets brand reality. The optimization statement determines the mix.

### Optimizing for top of funnel

Weight toward **Trigger** formats.

Talk to your media buyer about making these **link-click or traffic optimized**, not conversion optimized. Save conversion optimization for everything else.

You need a wide net: short-form video, pattern interrupt, novelty. Get in front of as many pre-aware people as possible and let the moments do the work. Distribute the rest of the funnel evenly across Exploration, Evaluation, and Purchase.

### Optimizing for sales growth

Weight toward **Evaluation** and **Purchase**.

Growth optimization is not top-of-funnel growth, and the creative and account structure should reflect that. You need depth: more proof, more specificity, more trust. Generally not a place for short hooks (though they occasionally land).

### Optimizing for cost reduction

Choose formats your team can produce **at extremely high quality.**

Quality doesn't mean a movie. Quick-and-dirty UGC still counts. It means: **as close as possible to the best story you can tell.** Put the effort in the middle of the structure.

&gt; **Quality is a cost reducer.**

Tell a genuinely good story and people buy. Make them laugh and they buy. Relatable, high-quality content draws in customers who align their *identity* with the brand — and that is a much cheaper sale.

## Where to research presentation

| Need | Go to | Look for |

|---|---|---|

| What formats work in your category | Motion's inspiration library, Meta Ads Library, TikTok Creative Center | Top-performing formats by category. **Filter for state, not for trending hooks.** |

| What competitors are running | Meta Ads Library longevity filter | 30+ days is a signal — **then check the story** |

| How customers consume content | Google Analytics content paths, heat maps, YouTube Analytics | Watch times, drop-off points, how deep they go on site |

| **Trends, stories, cultural knowledge** | **Organic** — Reels, Shorts, Twitter, LinkedIn, Pinterest | 30+ minutes a week, minimum |

| What's actually converting | Post-purchase surveys, customer quizzes, performance data | **Which creative drove purchases** — not click-through rate |

### Three cautions

**Longevity is a signal, not proof.** A 500-day ad might be genuinely great. Or a brand may be parking spend on a hundred ads specifically to confuse competitors watching the library. Go with your gut: is it well-produced? Good story, good creator, good delivery? If yes, keep it. If the concept makes no sense, ignore the runtime.

**Spend real time on organic.** This is where the majority of presentation research should happen. Organic creators *have* to be interesting to get what they need from the algorithm — that skill set tells you which narratives and storytelling forms are about to matter. Especially now that Andromeda is picky about what it serves.

**Click-through rate lies.** A click is not a customer. High CTR with weak conversion means something in the message is pulling the wrong brain, and you need more data before you scale it.

## The tagging system

**Tag every idea you source with its TEEP phase.** Trigger, Exploration, Evaluation, or Purchase.

This builds something rare: an ad library that is not just *usable* but **browsable** — indexed by customer reality and by brand reality simultaneously.

**The payoff.** When a concept dies mid-month, you don't start a search. You go to the library and immediately pull the right format, for the right customer phase, at the right brand optimization level.

That's what strategy looks like operationally. The alternative is scrambling for new concepts while performance decays.
