## The test

Before anything else, ask how well the strategist actually knows this person.

Not demographics. Not the psychographic avatar the team generated three years ago.

- Do you know someone in your life who matches this profile?

- Do you know what her Tuesday at 2pm looks like?

- Do you know the specific moment in her week when she becomes aware something in her life isn't working?

If not, you don't know her. You know *about* her.

You can have a perfect brand, sophisticated media buying, and the most beautiful ads ever produced. If they talk to the wrong person, or to the wrong *moment* in that person's life, the house falls down.

## Layer 1: generational insights

**Why bother.** The brain is a pattern recognizer. All it does is map new things onto everything it has already experienced. Childhood shapes the teens, which shape the twenties and thirties, which shape the forties and fifties — automatically, without permission. Write to who someone is today and you're ignoring the map their brain is actually running.

**Understanding the generation tells you which formats feel safe, and how to bypass the brain's "no" filter.**

The **familiarity effect**: the brain processes familiar stimuli faster, because it resists them less. If your customer grew up reading long-form magazine articles, an article-format ad may outperform a TikTok-style hook — purely on familiarity.

Familiar formats worth keeping in your library: magazine articles, nutrition labels, price stickers, CAT scans, receipts, text threads. Identify which ones *this* customer has seen ten thousand times.

### The four questions

**1. What was their childhood informational environment?**

Three broadcast TV channels, where every message was authoritative and singular? Or infinite internet, where they learned early that everything is negotiable and everyone has an agenda?

→ Tells you which formats feel safe and how much trust-building the ad must do before it can say anything.

**2. What shaped their relationship with money?**

Coming of age in the 1990s boom produces a different response than graduating into the 2008 crisis and spending your twenties on student debt.

*"Self-care starts at home"* lands as aspiration for one person and as an insult for another — *I can barely afford rent and you want me to do self-care?*

→ Find out what they believe about money, specifically inside your category.

**3. What is their actual life right now?**

Not the aspirational life. The real one. Building a career or pivoting out of one? Sandwiched between aging parents and teenagers? Newly retired and struggling with the loss of structure?

→ This is where creative tension lives, and it's what makes an ad relevant *today*, on a specific Tuesday — not relevant in general.

And it's where empathy lives. Life is hard for everyone. Knowing where someone is lets you slot into their life where you actually fit, rather than constantly trying to extract something from them.

**4. How do they evaluate risk and decide whom to trust?**

Authority-averse — no governments, no institutions, no experts? Push everything toward peer and friend angles.

Authority-trusting — doctors, credentials, institutions? Shift toward endorsement and credential.

Gen Z and millennials watched institutions fail repeatedly and lean toward subreddits and peer reviews. Gen X and boomers often lean toward physician endorsement and clinical backing. **Same product, same claims, completely different creative architecture.**

### What this layer is for

**Not angles.** Do not write down angles here. This is a library you're building in your own head — you are becoming a registered expert on the chosen human. It's for you, not yet for the brand.

Absorb the vocabulary, the trust patterns, the money story. Later, drafting or prompting, you'll be able to say: *based on what I know about this person, they wouldn't love that headline. They'd love this one.*

## Where to research generations

| Need | Go to | Look for |

|---|---|---|

| Generational worldview | Pew Research Center, academic generational studies | Economic events they lived through, tech adoption timing, trust patterns → proof formats, authority signals |

| How they talk about daily life | Reddit (age-specific subreddits), Facebook groups, Quora, LinkedIn | Language patterns, complaints, what they celebrate vs. what frustrates them |

| Cultural and economic forces | Bureau of Labor Statistics, census data, news archives | Cost-of-living shifts, career patterns, family structure trends, **how your category's prices moved over 10 years** |

| Real conversation patterns | YouTube comment sections, podcast reviews, Amazon Q&amp;As | Tone, vocabulary, ambient skepticism level |

That third row is the sleeper. Category price history reveals **hidden objections** people will never volunteer in a post-purchase survey. If your product's category went from $2 to $70 in six years, "treat yourself" will backfire and nobody will tell you why.

## Layer 2: micro-moments

&gt; **A good strategist finds pain points. A great strategist finds out when those pain points show up, and why.**

Not pain points in the abstract. The exact moment in the year, the week, the day when *"I have a problem"* gets triggered. **Real-world situations, not marketing situations.**

**The example.** A 57-year-old woman and a weight-loss supplement.

- *Fun fact:* she wants to lose weight. Every competitor knows this.

- *Micro-moment:* she's getting dressed for lunch with a friend who just lost forty pounds. Her jeans don't fit the way they used to. She doesn't know how she compares. She's anxious before she's even out the door.

The second one gives you somewhere to put the camera, a creator to cast, a story to tell, and a line to write.

**Why it matters more now.** Andromeda wants hyper-specific content — Levinger reads it as an interest-based algorithm rather than purely angle- or customer-based. Hyper-specific situations are the unit it rewards.

---

## Where to research micro-moments

| Need | Go to | Look for |

|---|---|---|

| Trigger moments, daily frictions | Reddit, Facebook groups, niche forums | First-person stories. *"I was at the dentist." "I was shopping with my niece."* **These become ad scripts verbatim.** |

| Emotional language about the problem | Amazon reviews, Trustpilot | Before/after descriptions, what finally made them buy, what they wish they'd known. Compare 5-star vs. 3-star vs. 1-star to find objections. |

| Unfiltered customer voice | Quiz data, customer service tickets, post-purchase surveys | **Why now** — not why in general. What tipped them from objection into purchase. |

| Social comparison moments | TikTok and Instagram comments, organic | What people tag each other in. *"This is so me."* Tells you what they're already sensitive to. |

Prefer sources where people give opinions freely and un-incentivized.

## Layer 3: emotional mapping

For **each** micro-moment you've collected, three questions:

**1. Which emotion is present, most strongly?**

Humans experience emotions at wildly different intensities, and often several at once. Which one you identify drastically changes the ad and whether the market responds.

**2. Is it private or social?**

Does she deal with this alone, or in front of people? This changes the message entirely.

**3. Is it acute or chronic?**

Did it arrive suddenly, or has she been living with it for years?

### Why these three change everything

Take hope, private, chronic. She's been struggling alone, for years, and you want to offer hope.

- *"Join others just like you"* — too hype-y. It presumes she wants to be seen.

- *"You don't have to suffer alone anymore"* — softer, quieter, correct.

Nearly identical messages. They pull entirely different customers.

**The shift this represents.** Creative strategy used to ask *which formats, which angles, which offers.* It now asks: **how do we need to communicate this message so the person actually understands what we're saying?**

## Valence and intensity

The model that organizes which emotion to target and how.

Two axes:

- **Valence** — positive (above the line) or negative (below)

- **Intensity** — how strongly felt

Four quadrants. Where an emotion sits tells you which headline, which hook, which story to build. A high-intensity negative emotion demands a completely different construction than a low-intensity positive one.

**Attribution matters here.** The model is psychologist **James A. Russell's (1980)** circumplex model of affect. Levinger applied it to ads; she does not claim the model. Cite it correctly.
