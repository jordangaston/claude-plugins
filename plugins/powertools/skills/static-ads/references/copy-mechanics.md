# Copy Mechanics

The eight mechanics behind winning static copy, with the failure mode for each.

A top-performing static uses **at least one.** The best use two or three. Using all eight produces an ad with no focal point — which fails Layer 2 regardless of how good the copy is.

## The eight mechanics

### 1. Specificity

Generic always loses. The move is to name the demographic, give exact numbers, and lead with real outcomes.

**The strongest form:** lead with a number that communicates **time, effort, or cost.**

- *"5 minutes."*

- *"Under $5."*

- *"2 inches in one use."*

**Failure mode:** "Save time and money." Nobody has ever bought anything because of that sentence.

---

### 2. Direct audience callout

Name them. *"Nurses." "Second-year associates." "Anyone who's ever thrown out a shirt because of pit stains."*

**Self-selection is a copy mechanic.** It performs targeting inside the creative, independent of the ad set — which matters increasingly as platform targeting collapses toward broad.

**Failure mode:** calling out a group so large it selects nobody. "Busy parents" is a census category. "Parents who've eaten dinner standing up three nights this week" is a callout.

---

### 3. Taboo

Say what your competitors are too polite to say.

Two effects, and the second is the more valuable one:

- It stops the scroll

- **It identifies with the persona at a much deeper level** — the reader feels seen because someone finally said the thing

**Where it lives:** the headliner format. Nothing should compete with the sentence.

**Failure mode:** edginess without recognition. Taboo works when it names something the reader already thinks and hasn't heard aloud. Shock without recognition is just noise.

---

### 4. Primal desire over product benefit

The list: **status, sex, belonging, safety, approval.**

**The product is just the mechanism — it's how they get those things. The desire is the headline.**

If your headline names a feature, or even a benefit, you have skipped this step. "Non-staining formula" is a feature. "Never take your jacket off in a meeting again" is safety and status.

**Failure mode:** benefit-laddering that stops one rung short. Most brands climb from feature to benefit and stop, because the desire feels too naked to say out loud. Say it.

---

### 5. Curiosity loop

Show the setup, hide the payoff behind the click.

**The obligation this creates:** if a curiosity ad becomes a top performer, **the landing page must satisfy the loop.** An unclosed loop is a bounce, and it will show up as good CTR with terrible CPA.

**Failure mode:** curiosity with no payoff anywhere in the funnel. This is clickbait, and the metrics will flatter you before they punish you.

---

### 6. Negative marketing

State it in the negative. Name what the audience is afraid of.

This is the same psychological engine as taboo but aimed at fear rather than at social convention. "Your deodorant is ruining your shirts" is negative marketing. It works because it's *true and unsaid*.

**Failure mode:** manufactured fear about a problem the reader doesn't have. Negative marketing only works when the fear is already sitting there.

---

### 7. Borrow your customers' language

**The best copy often isn't written at all.**

Hunt for **golden nugget testimonials** — the reviews that would work verbatim as advertising. This produces winners repeatedly, more reliably than most original copywriting.

Method in the next section.

**Failure mode:** cleaning up the grammar. The imperfection is the credibility.

---

### 8. Show the transformation

Don't describe the after state. **Show it.** This is why before/after works.

Assertion asks to be believed. Depiction is received before the skeptical apparatus engages.

**On legality:** before/after ads are not illegal. Restrictions are tighter for **cosmetic procedures and weight-loss products** — check policy there specifically, and don't let the restriction leak into categories it doesn't cover.

**Failure mode:** a "before" that's obviously staged. The credibility of the after depends entirely on the before.

## Golden nugget testimonials

The highest-yield, lowest-effort copy source available.

**The method:** export a CSV of all your reviews. Hand it to an LLM. Ask it to surface the ones that would work in advertising.

**A prompt that works:**

&gt; Here are all our customer reviews. Find me the "golden nugget" testimonials — the ones that would work verbatim as ad copy. I'm looking for: reviews where someone describes a specific transformation, reviews that name an emotion rather than a feature, reviews where the phrasing is unusual or surprising, and reviews where a skeptic describes what changed their mind.

&gt;

&gt; Quote them verbatim. Do not clean up the grammar. Do not paraphrase.

**What to do with them:**

- Use one as the **headline** of a benefits-callout ad

- Build a **headliner** around the single best sentence

- Use them as the copy in a **text-only** ad

**What not to do:** review-quote-heavy collages. Stacking many testimonials into one graphic consistently turns out badly — it violates visual hierarchy and gives the eye nowhere to land. One quote, big. Not eight quotes, small.

## Headline patterns

The headline does the targeting. Two things always worth testing:

1. **Core desire vs. generic.** Swap a generic headline for one naming status, belonging, safety, sex, or approval.

2. **A golden nugget testimonial as the headline.** Let the customer write it.

Structural patterns that recur in winners:

| Pattern | Shape | Mechanic |

|---|---|---|

| The number | *"2 inches in one use"* | Specificity |

| The callout | *"Nurses: stop doing this"* | Self-selection |

| The unsaid thing | *"Your deodorant is the problem"* | Taboo + negative |

| The reversal | *"Your résumé is fine. The system isn't."* | Curiosity + negative |

| The verbatim | *"I never thought I'd taste that again"* | Borrowed language |

## Diagnosing weak copy

Ask in this order:

**Which of the eight is this using?** If the honest answer is "none," that's the finding. Stop there.

**Does the headline name a desire or a feature?** Features are the most common failure and the easiest to fix.

**Is the specificity real?** Numbers, named demographics, concrete outcomes — or adjectives?

**Would the persona recognize their own vocabulary?** If the copy sounds like the brand talking, it will be filtered as advertising. If it sounds like the customer talking, it gets read.

**Is it doing two jobs?** Copy that hedges between education and offer is copy that was briefed without a single goal. Send it back to Layer 1.
