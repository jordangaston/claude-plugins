# Static Ads

The craft layer for image creative, from Dara Denney's static-ad method. Where the `performance-creative` skill governs the *loop* (what to say, how to test), this governs the *artifact*.

## Three myths to kill on contact

Users arrive believing at least one of these. Correct it before doing the work, because each one produces a different bad ad.

**"Statics are for retargeting and bottom-of-funnel only."**

False. Zero in on awareness level, know your persona, and know exactly how they talk on social — you can write static messaging that reaches cold audiences. Statics are scalable top-of-funnel creative, not a support format.

**"Video is the only thing that works for my brand."**

This is a skill issue, and it has a specific cause: thinking about statics from the perspective of *format* rather than *awareness level*. Brands that fail at statics are usually skipping the messaging work and blaming the medium.

**"Statics are just for rapidly testing messaging so you can scale winners into video."**

This is the seductive one, and it's wrong. Format-swapping a working angle is genuinely good practice — but if you're only using images to pump out volume and get a read, you're missing what a great static can do to the ad account and to the business. The right static transforms brands. Statics are a destination, not a staging area.

&gt; **A note on the tension.** Denney's earlier public material leaned harder on statics-as-cheap-message-test, and that logic still holds for early concept discovery on small budgets: a static isolates one variable at ~1% of the cost of a video. Both can be true. The error the myth names is *treating the static as disposable once the message is found.* Test cheaply with statics; then build real statics.

## The three layers

Work them in order. Skipping Layer 1 is why most statics fail, and the failure shows up looking like a design problem.

### Layer 1 — Strategy (before a single line of copy)

Four decisions, all made before anyone opens a design tool.

**1. Pick one goal.** Offer ad? Education? Reaching a problem-aware buyer? **Pick one.** Do not make one ad do two jobs. This is the single most common way statics get ruined — trying to speak to too many people at once.

**2. Pick the persona — narrowly.** Not "small business owners." *Which* small business owner, at what stage, with what objection.

Persona determines three things downstream:

- the **vocabulary** you'll use
- the **proof** you'll need to supply
- the **objections** they're actively waiting for you to answer

(A retargeting ad speaking to multiple personas is a legitimate choice. A top-of-funnel killer static is not. That's a conscious fork.)

**3. Pick the format.** Format is not decoration. It decides *what copy can survive*, *what the image has to carry*, and *which copywriting frameworks are even viable.* See `references/[formats.md](http://formats.md)`.

**4. Match a level of awareness.** This is the bridge that connects persona and goal to the framework. Unaware → Problem Aware → Solution Aware → Product Aware → Most Aware.

If the user cannot answer all four, do not write copy. Ask.

### Layer 2 — Design

**Visual hierarchy.** The headline generally comes first — *the headline does the targeting.* Then the product or key visual. Then supporting elements.

The focal point — where the eye lands first — should usually be the messaging. Sometimes the image stops the scroll and sends the eye up to the copy. But nine times out of ten the focal point is the headline or the key visual, and most of the time you're aiming for the text.

**The one-second comprehension test.** *Clarity always outbeats creativity.* If a stranger cannot tell what you're selling, or what message you're communicating, within one second — kill it. Not fix it. Kill it.

The two failure modes, in order of frequency:

- **Too much going on.** No focal point, no hierarchy. This ad has four things competing and communicates none.
- **Too much text.** The bones are fine; the ad is illegible at thumbnail size.

Test at thumbnail size, on a phone, at arm's length. Not on a monitor.

### Layer 3 — Copy (the most important layer)

This is where the messaging is crafted for the persona. **At bare minimum, a top-performing static does at least one of the following.** The best do two or three, never all eight.

**1. Be specific. Generic always loses.**

Name the demographic. Give exact numbers. Lead with real outcomes. The strongest move is a number communicating time, effort, or cost: *"5 minutes." "Under $5." "2 inches in one use."*

**2. Call out the audience by name.**

Self-selection is a copy mechanic. It sharpens targeting inside the creative itself, independent of the ad set.

**3. Lean into the taboo.**

Say what your competitors are too polite to say. This stops the scroll *and* identifies with the persona at a much deeper level — the second effect matters more than the first.

**4. Tap a primal desire, not a product benefit.**

Status, sex, belonging, safety, approval. **The product is just the mechanism — the desire is the headline.** When a headline names a feature, it has skipped this step.

**5. Open a curiosity loop.**

Show the setup, hide the payoff behind the click. If one of these becomes a top performer, make sure the curiosity gets satisfied on the landing page. An unsatisfied loop is a bounce.

**6. Use negative marketing.**

Name what the audience is afraid of. State it in the negative.

**7. Borrow your customers' words.**

The best copy often isn't written at all. Hunt "golden nugget testimonials" — run a CSV of your reviews through an LLM and ask it to surface the ones that would work in advertising. This produces winners repeatedly.

**8. Show the transformation. Don't describe it.**

Depict the after state rather than asserting it. This is why before/after works.

&gt; **Before/after is not illegal.** People still claim this. It's false. There *are* meaningfully tighter restrictions around cosmetic procedures and weight-loss products — check policy for those categories specifically, and don't generalize the restriction to everything.

## Formats

Three production levels, then seven formats. Full detail with selection guidance in `references/[formats.md](http://formats.md)`.

**Production levels:**

- **Graphic-style** — designed, typographic
- **Hi-fi production** — studio photography
- **Lo-fi creator content** — *the one brands forget.* Creator-shot stills. Currently among the strongest performers in live accounts.

When someone says "statics" and means only graphic-style, name the gap.

**The seven formats to always be testing:**

| Format | What it's for |

|---|---|

| Educational infographic | Cold, top-of-funnel. Masquerades as organic content. |

| Headliner | Messaging is the focal point. The home of taboo. |

| Benefits callout | Product + annotated benefits. Ogilvy-era, still cranking. |

| Comparison (us vs. them) | Solution-aware. Still working. |

| Transformation | **The biggest gap in most accounts.** Unlocks problem/solution-aware. |

| Grid | Apparel, multi-SKU, sale periods. |

| Text-only / founder's letter | Borrows an organic format. Clients get nervous; it performs. |

**Transformation deserves emphasis.** It's the format most often missing, and it addresses the buyer who is problem-aware or solution-aware and simply *looking for proof.* They don't need to be convinced there's a problem. They need an answer.

## Creative diversity

**Different visuals for a working message is the cheapest volume you can buy.** If a piece of messaging is performing, port it across visual formats. That's how you get both volume and better performance without new research.

But diversity is not decoration — it's a gap analysis. Ask three questions of any ad account:

1. Which **awareness levels** are we failing to target?
2. Which **personas** are we failing to target?
3. Which **formats** are absent from the library?

The answers are the brief. This is the same audit you'd run against a competitor's ad library — what archetypes are present, what's missing, and where the market's sophistication level actually sits.

Market sophistication matters here: an established brand and a new brand playing the same format are playing different games. A competitor's top-performing comparison ads don't automatically mean you should run comparison ads.

## Critiquing an ad

When a user shows you a static — theirs or a competitor's — work the layers backwards. This is the fastest path to a useful answer.

**Design first, because it's the quickest kill.**

- Does it pass the one-second test?
- Is there a single focal point, or four things competing?
- Is there too much text to read at thumbnail size?

**Then copy.**

- Which of the eight mechanics is it using? If none: that's the finding.
- Does the headline name a desire or a feature?
- Is the specificity real (numbers, named demographic) or vague?

**Then strategy — the diagnosis that actually matters.**

- What single goal is this ad doing? Is it trying to do two?
- Who is the persona, and would they recognize their own vocabulary here?
- What awareness level does this assume, and does the offer match?

**Say "kill it" when it's true.** The one-second test is a kill test, not a revision test. An ad with no focal point and no hierarchy doesn't get fixed. It gets scrapped.

## Working with AI generation

If a user is generating statics with an AI tool, three things hold regardless of platform:

**Research before production.** Have the tool analyze the brand, the competitors, and the existing ad library *first.* It produces better output with more context — and the research itself teaches you something. A creative gap analysis (missing awareness levels, missing personas, missing formats) is the correct first artifact, not an ad.

**Three variations at a time.** Output quality degrades past that.

**Expect to edit, and know what to look for.** Every output is not usable — no honest person claims otherwise. The recurring failures worth catching:

- Too many elements; no visual hierarchy (Layer 2 violation)
- Too much text
- Review-quote-heavy collages — these consistently turn out poorly
- Typos and stray em-dashes in generated headline copy

The value of a tool is not its first output. It's whether you can iterate to something you'd actually launch.
